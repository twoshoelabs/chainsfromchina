Method

How this site counts China-origin retail and food & beverage chains open in the United States — what is measured, what is in scope, how closings and coverage work, and where every figure comes from.

What this is

A daily census of mainland-China-origin retail and F&B chains with storefronts open in the United States. The headline number is a count of real open outlets, not a forecast. Nothing is estimated unless it is marked with a dagger (†); everything else is something we or the chain actually observed.

How a store gets counted

A chain earns a place the day it lists a US store on its own store locator, or the day we hand-verify an outlet that has no readable locator. Each outlet is counted once, under the state it sits in. We count storefronts people can walk into — an unstaffed vending machine (a POP MART “roboshop,” say) is kept in the data but never added to the count.

Two kinds of figure sit behind the count. A collected chain is read every morning from its own locator, and the site can show you the raw capture for that day. A sighting is a location we confirmed by hand — used where a chain has no machine-readable locator (its list loads only in a browser, sits behind a signed app request, or doesn’t exist yet). Both are real outlets; only the first refreshes itself.

What’s in scope

Chains that originated in mainland China. Taiwan- and Hong-Kong-origin brands are a different, earlier wave and are out of scope — so Gong Cha, Tiger Sugar and The Alley are not here, and a brand that bills itself as Taiwanese is set aside even when it was built in Shanghai. We track storefront chains, not products on a grocery shelf, and not one-off flagships or showrooms — a chain is a chain.

When a store closes

A verified closure comes off the open list and is logged with its date. For a collected chain the daily census notices a closure by absence — a store drops off the chain’s own locator and, after a few collected days, is dated closed. When a chain’s locator keeps listing a store that has actually closed, we hold it closed anyway and note the gap: a brand’s own list overstating its footprint is itself a signal, the quiet half of “open loudly, close quietly.”

Putting it on the map

Addresses are turned into coordinates with the US Census Bureau’s geocoder; where its data has a gap (a new block, a mall-unit address) we add a coordinate by hand, derived from a neighbouring match or the chain’s own listing, and mark it so the map draws it as a derived point. A handful of outlets with an address we can’t yet place are counted but not drawn. Alaska and Hawaii sit in their own insets, at their own scale.

How complete is this?

How complete a chain’s count is depends on the chain, not on how we count it — a figure reached by hand is still a measured figure, not an estimate. For a chain we collect, the number is pulled each morning from its own full US locator, so it is the chain’s own count. For a chain we track by hand, we confirm each outlet individually from official listings and verified sightings; for most smaller chains that covers every US location, so the hand count is a complete census and we treat it as correct. A hand count trails the truth only when a chain opens faster than we re-check it — and where a parent company or the trade press states a US figure, we show it beside ours so any gap is visible.

The international register

The US map is measured; the international register is researched. It records which China-origin chains have opened outside China — read out of company filings, exchange announcements and the trade press, and typed in by hand, each row carrying the date it was true and how far to trust it. A count that was never published is left blank rather than guessed, and “no evidence” means a search turned up nothing, not that a chain is absent.

Sources & honesty

Figures come from first-party and official sources: a chain’s own store locator, company filings and press, franchise disclosure documents, government permit records, and locations we verify by hand. We do not scrape review, delivery or marketplace platforms for counts. Every number carries a date and goes stale; a modeled or estimated figure is always marked with a dagger (†) and kept on the far side of a clear line from what was measured.

Pro

The map and the daily counts are free. Deeper analysis — per-outlet and total revenue models, co-tenancy clusters, and capacity reality-checks — sits behind Pro, always on the modeled side of that line and always sourced.
